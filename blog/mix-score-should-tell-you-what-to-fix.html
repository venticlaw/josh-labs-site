Your Mix Sucks

A mix score should tell you what to fix.

A big number feels nice. A useful number tells the producer where to move next.

The dangerous version of a mix score is a sticker. Ninety-six out of one hundred. Looks official. Feels good. Teaches nothing.

Your Mix Sucks is meant to be harsher than that because producers do not need compliments from a plugin. They need a useful fight before the bounce leaves the room.

The score needs context

A short clip with noise, silence, or only one section should not be treated like a finished record. A podcast, a TikTok clip, and a full song do not deserve the same assumptions either. Media type changes what "good" means.

That is why the product points users toward categories: frequency, loudness, stereo, dynamics, and references. The score is the headline. The categories are the work.

If the notes say the mix has problems, the score should not pretend the mix is fine.

Good feedback names the decision

A useful diagnosis does not stop at "bad low end." It says whether the issue looks like sub build-up, low-mid masking, harsh presence, missing air, stereo imbalance, loudness risk, or dynamics that feel flat.

That is the difference between a score and a coach. The plugin has to make the next decision easier, even when the answer hurts a little.

Why loudness standards matter

ITU-R BS.1770 and EBU R 128 are widely used references for measuring programme loudness. Spotify also gives creator-facing normalization guidance. Those standards do not mix the song for you, but they explain why loudness and true peak are not vibes. They are measurable parts of the final playback experience.
